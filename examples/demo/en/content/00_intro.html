③ Independence & conditional independence: what the Naive Bayes page is betting on

Two events are independent when knowing one occurred tells you nothing about the other: , equivalently .

Conditional independence is subtler: . Meaning — and may be correlated, but once Y is known, the correlation vanishes.

An analogy: "the ground is wet" and "people carry umbrellas" are strongly correlated (seeing one raises the odds of the other). Neither causes the other — the real common cause is "it's raining". So given "raining", the two events become independent. That is conditional independence.

The Naive Bayes assumption on page 17 is exactly this: all features are conditionally independent given the class. It is usually false (page 31 openly mocks it), but the payoff is enormous — page 26 does the arithmetic.

⑦ Vectors, dot products & transpose: what actually computes

Logistic regression revolves around one quantity:

This is the dot product (inner product) of two vectors; the result is a single number. Read it as: multiply each feature by its weight and sum. A positive weight means growing that feature tends to grow the result; a near-zero weight means the feature hardly matters.

The superscript is the transpose: it lays a column vector down into a row vector, so a row times a column has matching dimensions and yields a number (not a matrix).

So is just the school-era "weighted sum plus offset", written in scary vector notation. When it equals zero, you have the classification boundary — the page-47 conclusion.

⑧ Convex & concave: why "concave" is good news

A convex function looks like a bowl (opening up): a ball inside always rolls to the unique lowest point. A concave function is an upside-down bowl: the ball always rolls to the unique highest point.

Why the shape matters: there are no "fake" local optima. Start climbing from anywhere, keep going up, and you must reach the global maximum — you cannot get stuck in a small dip.

Page 53 says "bad news: no closed form; good news: is concave" — meaning: although no formula hands you the answer, an iterative method is guaranteed to converge to the unique optimum.

The 3-D surface on page 54 shows (negating a concave function gives a bowl-shaped convex one); the red dots are the iterates rolling downhill.
