This is the cover of the deck: the course Introduction to Artificial Intelligence, today's topic "Logistic Regression", taught by Su Hang (Tsinghua University).

There is no new content on this page, but it sets the tone — the next 62 pages develop one of the most fundamental and time-tested classification models in machine learning. Logistic regression is still widely used today in spam filtering, click-through-rate prediction, credit scoring, and disease-risk estimation; and the machinery behind it (maximum likelihood + gradient methods) is the prototype of almost every modern neural-network training procedure.

How to read the title: keep two words in mind — logistic (referring to the Sigmoid function) and regression (despite the name, it actually performs classification). This awkward naming is explained on page 46.

This page is a map: three questions that any probabilistic model must answer. No formulas here, but worth memorizing — every subsequent page is answering one of them.

1. Representation

"How to capture/model uncertainty in possible worlds?" "How to encode our domain knowledge / assumptions / constraints?" — how to write uncertainty down as a mathematical object, and how to inject prior knowledge into it? This deck's answers: represent uncertainty with probability distributions (joint, conditional); inject assumptions via the Naive Bayes assumption and Beta priors.
